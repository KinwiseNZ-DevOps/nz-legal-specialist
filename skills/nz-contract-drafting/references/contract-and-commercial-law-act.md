Contents: what the CCLA 2017 is, contractual privity, mistake, misrepresentation and cancellation, electronic transactions.

Source: Contract and Commercial Law Act 2017 (CCLA), legislation.govt.nz. Verify current section numbers before citing; this is a consolidating Act that replaced several older Acts (including the Contractual Mistakes Act 1977, Contractual Remedies Act 1979, Contracts (Privity) Act 1982, Illegal Contracts Act 1970, Sale of Goods Act 1908, and the Electronic Transactions Act 2002), so pre-2017 citations are wrong and must not be used.

## Part 2, Subpart 1: Contractual privity (ss 10-20)

- **s 12** - a person who is not a party to a deed or contract can enforce a promise made for their benefit, if the deed or contract shows an intention that the promise is enforceable by that beneficiary.
- **s 14** - varying or discharging a promise that benefits a third party may need that beneficiary's consent once their position has changed in reliance on it.
- **s 17** - how a beneficiary enforces the promise; **s 18** - defences available against the beneficiary are generally the same as those available against the promisee.
- Drafting check: a contract naming an intended third-party beneficiary (e.g. a guarantee for an affiliate, a warranty running to a successor) should say so expressly if that is the intent, or expressly exclude third-party enforcement if it is not (a standard "no third party has any right under this contract except as expressly stated" clause engages s 12's own-intention test correctly).

## Part 2, Subpart 2: Contractual mistakes (ss 21-32)

- **s 24** - a court may grant relief where one party's mistake was known to the other party, or the mistake was common or mutual to both parties, and specified conditions are met.
- **s 25** - a mistake in interpreting the contract is not the kind of mistake this subpart covers; that is a matter of ordinary contract interpretation.
- **s 26** - no relief if the party seeking it was aware of the mistake and decided to enter the contract anyway.
- Drafting check: do not rely on this subpart to fix a badly worded clause; mistake relief is about a false shared or known assumption of fact, not about a term being unclear.

## Part 2, Subpart 3: Contractual remedies (ss 33-51)

- **s 35** - damages for misrepresentation: a party induced to enter a contract by a misrepresentation (even an innocent one) may recover damages as if the representation were a term of the contract, subject to the Act.
- **s 37** - a party may cancel the contract if induced to enter it by a misrepresentation, if a term is or will be breached in a way that substantially reduces the benefit of the contract, or in other specified circumstances.
- **s 38** - a contract once affirmed (with knowledge of the right to cancel) cannot then be cancelled for the same reason.
- **s 50** - a clause purporting to prevent a court inquiring into a statement, promise or undertaking made during negotiations, or into the authority of a person to make it, does not have that effect except to the extent the court considers fair and reasonable in the circumstances. This is the section that limits the effect of standard "entire agreement" and "no reliance" clauses.
- Drafting check: an entire-agreement clause is still useful for certainty, but do not draft or accept one as if it fully extinguishes misrepresentation liability; flag this limit to the client and consider whether a specific, accurate warranty schedule is the safer way to manage pre-contract statements.

## Part 4: Electronic transactions (ss 207-231)

- **s 211** - information is not denied legal validity only because it is in electronic form.
- **s 219-s 221** - a legal requirement can be met by electronic means where the parties have consented (express or reasonably inferred) and the method is as reliable as appropriate, and the information's integrity is maintained.
- **s 220** - consent to use electronic technology is needed; it should not be assumed from silence.
- **s 226** - a legal requirement for a signature can be met electronically if the method reliably identifies the signatory and indicates their approval, and is as reliable as appropriate for the purpose.
- Drafting check: confirm the document type is one this Part actually covers before relying on an e-signature clause; some documents (certain deeds, some land and property transactions) carry extra formal requirements under other Acts that Part 4 does not override. Flag this as a point to confirm rather than asserting electronic execution is always valid.
